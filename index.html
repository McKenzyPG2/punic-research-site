How did communities express belonging?

How did communities express civic, religious, institutional and other forms of collective belonging across Phoenician–Punic colonial and contact societies, and how did these expressions vary across time and place?

People could express belonging through cities, institutions, religious communities, occupations, local traditions and political relationships. A shared language, cult or material tradition does not automatically establish a uniform ethnic identity.

Working method

Make the inference inspectable

Computer-assisted research can connect a claim to a located passage, its edition, object, date and review state. The aim is to make observations comparable and testable at scale while preserving the distinctions that source criticism requires.

Implemented experiments

Delos uses deterministic extraction and provisional in-sample diagnostics. Lepcis presents selected annotations from an actual LLM run: 26 of 26 quotations matched supplied source lines. Dates and cautionary instructions were provided to the model; this is quotation integrity, not independently validated historical accuracy.

Carthage adds an interactive photograph viewer: recorded visual proposals, withheld labels and catalogue disagreements remain connected to their object and location.

Proposed research

Controlled comparisons could test how civic, religious and institutional expressions depend on genre, chronology and context. Expert-reviewed references, annotation completeness, abstention and held-out monuments would be needed before measuring generalization.

Historical programme

Which differences need a historical explanation?

Quinn’s In Search of the Phoenicians (2018) questions a uniform overarching category; Demetriou’s Phoenicians among Others (2023) investigates migrants, institutions and overlapping affiliations, including western communities. These provide starting points, not an empty field awaiting an AI solution.

The proposed contribution is a bounded explanation of when affiliations mattered to membership, obligations or institutional participation—after accounting for genre, chronology, language, indigenous agency and unequal survival. New historical knowledge would come from source analysis and contextual comparison, not extraction scores. Neither historical nor methodological novelty is established.

Delos is a migrant-association pilot, not a western colony. Lepcis and Dougga supply different North African contexts. A wider corpus remains to be selected through feasibility and reuse audits.

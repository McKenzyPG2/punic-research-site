Punic words, Numidian institutions?

What does writing in Punic reveal about local community, royal affiliation and public office?

A bilingual dedication gives us a way to ask that question without equating language with identity. Read three expressions, then separate what is written from what the institutions might have been.

Selected Punic phrases from DEAPS, KAI 101. The edition displays Punic in Hebrew characters: these are not Hebrew-language passages or copies of the stone’s letterforms. Line numbers refer to the Punic text.

Local collective · KAI 101, Punic l. 1

Who acts?

Published transcription · Hebrew characters

בעלא תבגג

Our scholarly consonantal transliteration

bʿlʾ tbgg

Our provisional translation

“The citizens of Thugga”

Interpretation: a collective designation. “Citizens” leaves open which people it included; it does not establish everyone’s status or ethnicity.

Royal dedication · KAI 101, Punic l. 1

To whom?

Published transcription · Hebrew characters

למסנסנ הממלכת

Our scholarly consonantal transliteration

lmsnsn hmmlkt

Our provisional translation

“To Massinissa the king”

Interpretation: the royal recipient is explicit. The dedication raises questions about local relations to kingship; it does not answer how all inhabitants understood belonging.

Office · KAI 101, Punic l. 2

Which authority?

Published transcription · Hebrew characters

רבת מאת

Our scholarly consonantal transliteration

rbt mʾt

Our provisional translation

“Chiefs of the hundred”

Interpretation: an office title, not a verified job description. A literal gloss does not establish a council of exactly one hundred or a military command.

Latin represents consonants (ʾ = aleph, ʿ = ayin), not reconstructed pronunciation. English is our provisional rendering, informed by DEAPS’s attributed German translation; it is not a published English translation. These short phrases contain no restored letters; the wider text does.

Language choice is not institutional identity

Does Punic translate an institution—or make a local institution legible in another written register?

Chaker cautions that the Punic office expression is an adaptation of Libyan MWSN, not a literal translation that identifies its function. His analysis challenges a direct inference from Punic terminology to a Carthaginian institutional model. Chaker, “A propos de la terminologie libyque des titres et fonctions”, §2, online PDF pp. 2–3.

Shared administrative language, institutional borrowing and local adaptation are possible, overlapping explanations to investigate. The bilingual text makes the problem concrete; it does not establish ethnicity, population-wide bilingualism or identity transformation. This is established scholarly terrain, not a claim of discovery or novelty.
